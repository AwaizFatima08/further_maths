## Q1. Every numbered syllabus point and its exact learning outcomes for Paper 1 and Paper 2, the assumed prior knowledge, and the 2028–2030 changes

### Takeaway
The topic list the brief expected is **confirmed exactly**:
- **Paper 1** covers 1.1 Roots of polynomial equations, 1.2 Rational functions and graphs, 1.3 Summation of series, 1.4 Matrices, 1.5 Polar coordinates, 1.6 Vectors and 1.7 Proof by induction.
- **Paper 2** covers 2.1 Hyperbolic functions, 2.2 Matrices, 2.3 Differentiation, 2.4 Integration, 2.5 Complex numbers and 2.6 Differential equations.

Paper 2 assumes all of the Paper 1 content. Both papers assume 9709 Papers 1 and 3. The 2028–2030 syllabus says "There are no significant changes which affect teaching". The Paper 1 and Paper 2 content wording is the same in both syllabuses, apart from one example in 1.7.

### Cited Findings

**Content overview (2026–27, p.8).** The topics are 1.1 Roots of polynomial equations; 1.2 Rational functions and graphs; 1.3 Summation of series; 1.4 Matrices; 1.5 Polar coordinates; 1.6 Vectors; 1.7 Proof by induction; 2.1 Hyperbolic functions; 2.2 Matrices; 2.3 Differentiation; 2.4 Integration; 2.5 Complex numbers; 2.6 Differential equations — [2026–27 syllabus](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697357-2026-2027-syllabus.pdf)

Learners are expected to have studied "the majority of" 9709 before 9231 — [2026–27 syllabus](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697357-2026-2027-syllabus.pdf)

#### Paper 1: Further Pure Mathematics 1 (syllabus pp.16–19). Each item gives the learning outcome, then the notes and examples.

**1.1 Roots of polynomial equations**
- Recall and use the relations between the roots and coefficients of polynomial equations.
  - Notes: e.g. to evaluate symmetric functions of the roots, or to solve problems involving unknown coefficients; *restricted to equations of degree 2, 3 or 4 only*.
- Use a substitution to obtain an equation whose roots are related in a simple way to those of the original equation.
  - Notes: *substitutions will not be given for the easiest cases*, e.g. reciprocals, squares or a simple linear function of the old roots.

**1.2 Rational functions and graphs**
- Sketch graphs of simple rational functions, including the determination of oblique asymptotes, where the degree of the numerator and of the denominator is at most 2.
  - Notes: includes finding the set of values the function takes, e.g. by a discriminant. Detailed plotting is not required, but sketches should show significant features: turning points, asymptotes and intersections with the axes.
- Understand and use the relationships between the graphs of y = f(x), y² = f(x), y = 1/f(x) and y = |f(x)|.
  - Notes: includes using such sketches to solve equations or inequalities.

**1.3 Summation of series**
- Use the standard results for Σr, Σr², Σr³ to find related sums.
- Use the method of differences to obtain the sum of a finite series.
  - Notes: partial fractions may be needed to put the general term in a suitable form.
- Recognise, by direct consideration of a sum to n terms, when a series is convergent, and find the sum to infinity in such cases.

**1.4 Matrices**
- Carry out matrix addition, subtraction and multiplication, and recognise the terms zero matrix and identity (unit) matrix.
  - Notes: includes non-square matrices; at most 3 rows and 3 columns.
- Recall the meaning of 'singular' and 'non-singular' for square matrices. For 2×2 and 3×3 matrices, evaluate determinants and find inverses of non-singular matrices.
  - Notes: the notations det M and I are used.
- Understand and use (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹ for non-singular matrices.
  - Notes: may extend to the product of more than two matrices.
- Understand the use of 2×2 matrices for geometric transformations in the x-y plane:
  - the relationship between the transformations represented by A and A⁻¹;
  - AB represents B followed by A;
  - recall how the area scale factor relates to the determinant;
  - find the matrix for a given transformation or sequence of transformations.
  - Notes: 'rotation', 'reflection', 'enlargement', 'stretch' and 'shear' must be understood. Other 2D transformations may be included, but no particular knowledge of them is expected.
- Understand 'invariant' as applied to points and lines, and solve simple problems on invariant points and invariant lines.
  - Examples: the invariant points of (6 5; 2 3); the invariant lines through the origin for (4 −1; 2 1); showing that any line with gradient 1 is invariant for (2 0; 1 1).

**1.5 Polar coordinates**
- Understand the relations between Cartesian and polar coordinates, and convert equations of curves between the two forms.
  - Notes: *the convention r ≥ 0 is used*.
- Sketch simple polar curves for 0 ≤ θ < 2π, or −π < θ ≤ π, or a subset of either.
  - Notes: no detailed plotting. Show symmetry, intersections with the initial line, the form of the curve at the pole, and the least and greatest values of r.
- **Recall** the formula ½∫r² dθ for the area of a sector, and use it in simple cases.

**1.6 Vectors**
- Use the equation of a plane in any of the forms ax+by+cz=d, r·n=p or r=a+λb+μc, and convert between them.
- **Recall** that a×b = |a||b|sinθ n̂, or in component form (a₂b₃−a₃b₂)i + (a₃b₁−a₁b₃)j + (a₁b₂−a₂b₁)k.
- Use equations of lines and planes, with scalar and vector products, to solve problems on distances, angles and intersections, including:
  - whether a line lies in a plane, is parallel to it or intersects it, and the point of intersection;
  - the foot of the perpendicular from a point to a plane;
  - the angle between a line and a plane, and between two planes;
  - the line of intersection of two planes;
  - the shortest distance between two skew lines;
  - the common perpendicular to two skew lines.

**1.7 Proof by induction**
- Use mathematical induction to establish a given result.
  - Examples: Σr³ = ¼n²(n+1)²; uₙ = ½(1+3ⁿ⁻¹) for uₙ₊₁ = 3uₙ−1, u₁=1; a matrix-power formula for (4 −1; 6 −1)ⁿ; 3²ⁿ⁺² + 8n − 9 (or similar) divisible by 8. The divisibility example extracted as "3 2n+2 # 5n − 3 is divisible by 8"; check the exact expression in the PDF.
- Recognise when conjecture from a limited trial followed by inductive proof is a useful strategy, and carry it out in simple cases.
  - Examples: the nth derivative of x eˣ; Σ r × r!.

#### Paper 2: Further Pure Mathematics 2 (syllabus pp.20–23)

**2.1 Hyperbolic functions**
- Understand the definitions of sinh, cosh, tanh, sech, cosech and coth in terms of the exponential function.
- Sketch the graphs of hyperbolic functions.
- Prove and use identities involving hyperbolic functions.
  - Examples: cosh²x − sinh²x ≡ 1, sinh 2x ≡ 2 sinh x cosh x, and results matching the standard trigonometric identities.
- Understand and use the definitions of the inverse hyperbolic functions, and *derive* and use their logarithmic forms.

**2.2 Matrices**
- Formulate a problem with 3 linear simultaneous equations in 3 unknowns as a matrix equation, or vice versa.
- Understand the cases of consistency or inconsistency of 3 linear equations, relate them to the singularity of the matrix, solve consistent systems, and interpret geometrically in terms of lines and planes.
  - Examples: three planes meeting in a common point, in a common line, or with no common points.
- Understand 'characteristic equation', 'eigenvalue' and 'eigenvector' for square matrices.
  - Notes: includes using Ae = λe to prove simple properties, e.g. that λⁿ is an eigenvalue of Aⁿ.
- Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors of 2×2 and 3×3 matrices.
  - Notes: *restricted to real and distinct eigenvalues*.
- Express a square matrix as QDQ⁻¹, where D is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues and Q has eigenvectors as columns, and use this.
  - Examples: powers of 2×2 or 3×3 matrices.
- Use the fact that a square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation (Cayley–Hamilton; the syllabus does not use the name).
  - Examples: successive powers, or an inverse. Restricted to 2×2 or 3×3.

**2.3 Differentiation**
- Differentiate the hyperbolic functions, and sin⁻¹x, cos⁻¹x, sinh⁻¹x, cosh⁻¹x and tanh⁻¹x.
- Obtain an expression for d²y/dx² when x and y are related implicitly or parametrically.
- Derive and use the first few terms of a Maclaurin's series.
  - Notes: *no general term*, but successive 'implicit' differentiation may be needed, e.g. for y = tan x after rearranging to y′ = 1 + y².

**2.4 Integration**
- Integrate hyperbolic functions, and recognise integrals of 1/√(a²−x²), 1/√(x²+a²) and 1/√(x²−a²). Integrate associated functions with trigonometric or hyperbolic substitutions.
  - Notes: includes completing the square, e.g. ∫1/√(x²+x) dx.
- Derive and use reduction formulae to evaluate definite integrals.
  - Examples: ∫₀^{π/2} sinⁿx dx and ∫₀¹ e⁻ˣ(1−x)ⁿ dx. Harder cases may come with hints, e.g. ∫₀^{π/4} secⁿx dx by considering d/dx(tan x secⁿx).
- Understand how rectangles approximate the area under a curve, and use rectangles to estimate or bound the area, or to derive inequalities or limits for sums.
  - Notes: rectangles may have unit width or a width tending to zero. Examples: 1 + ln n > Σ_{r=1}^{n} 1/r > ln(n+1), and Σ (1/n)(1 + r/n)⁻¹ → ∫₀¹ (1+x)⁻¹ dx.
- Use integration to find:
  - arc lengths for curves in Cartesian form (including with a parameter) or in polar form;
  - surface areas of revolution about one of the axes for curves in Cartesian form (including with a parameter).
  - Notes: *surface areas of revolution for polar curves are not required*. Questions may need 9709 techniques in harder cases, e.g. ∫eˣ sin x dx by parts, or t = tan ½x.

**2.5 Complex numbers**
- Understand de Moivre's theorem for a positive or negative integer exponent, in terms of the geometrical effect of multiplying and dividing complex numbers.
- Prove de Moivre's theorem for a positive integer exponent, e.g. by induction.
- Use de Moivre's theorem for a positive or negative rational exponent:
  - to express trigonometrical ratios of multiple angles in terms of powers of ratios of the fundamental angle, e.g. cos 5θ in terms of cos θ, or tan 5θ in terms of tan θ;
  - to express powers of sin θ and cos θ in terms of multiple angles, e.g. sin⁶θ in terms of cos 2θ, cos 4θ and cos 6θ;
  - in the summation of series, e.g. the 'C + iS' method for Σ C(n,r) sin rθ;
  - in finding and using the nth roots of unity.

**2.6 Differential equations**
- Find an integrating factor for a first order linear differential equation and use it to find the general solution.
  - Examples: dy/dx − 2y = x²; x dy/dx − y = x⁴; dy/dx + y coth x = cosh x.
- Recall the meaning of 'complementary function' and 'particular integral', and that the general solution is CF + PI.
- Find the CF for a first or second order linear differential equation with constant coefficients.
  - Notes: covers distinct real, repeated real and complex conjugate auxiliary roots.
- Recall the form of, and find, a PI when a polynomial, a e^{bx}, or a cos px + b sin px is a suitable form. In other simple cases, find the coefficient(s) of a given PI form.
  - Example: find k given that kx cos 2x is a PI of y″ + 4y = sin 2x.
- Use a given substitution to reduce a differential equation to a first or second order linear equation with constant coefficients, or to a first order separable equation.
  - Examples: x = eᵗ for ax²y″ + bxy′ + cy; y = ux for dy/dx = (x+y)/(x−y).
- Use initial conditions to find a particular solution, and interpret a solution in terms of a modelled problem.

## Q2. MF19 formulae relevant to Papers 1 and 2, and what must be memorised

### Takeaway
MF19 is one shared list for 9709 and 9231, and "All formulae in the list may be needed for this syllabus." Its FURTHER PURE MATHEMATICS section gives:
- the Σr, Σr² and Σr³ results;
- the general Maclaurin series and standard Maclaurin expansions;
- the t = tan ½x results;
- three hyperbolic identities and the logarithmic forms of the inverse hyperbolic functions;
- derivatives of the inverse trigonometric, hyperbolic and inverse hyperbolic functions;
- a short table of integrals.

It does **not** give the polar sector area, arc length, surface area of revolution, the vector product, plane or distance formulae, the roots–coefficients relations, matrix results, de Moivre's theorem, or integrating-factor and CF/PI forms. Students must know these.

### Cited Findings
- "MF19 is a combined formulae list for AS & A Level Mathematics (9709) and AS & A Level Further Mathematics (9231). All formulae in the list may be needed for this syllabus." MF19 is supplied in the exam and reproduced in section 5 of the syllabus — [2026–27 syllabus p.30](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697357-2026-2027-syllabus.pdf)
- MF19 is marked "For use from 2020 in all papers for the above syllabuses" — [MF19](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/417318-list-of-formulae-and-statistical-tables.pdf)

**MF19, PURE MATHEMATICS section (from 9709, but available in 9231 exams)** — [MF19](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/417318-list-of-formulae-and-statistical-tables.pdf)
- **Mensuration:** sphere volume and surface area; cone volume and curved surface; arc length rθ; sector area ½r²θ.
- **Algebra:** quadratic formula; AP uₙ and Sₙ; GP uₙ, Sₙ and S∞ (|r|<1); binomial (a+b)ⁿ for positive integer n; (1+x)ⁿ for rational n, |x|<1.
- **Trigonometry:** tan = sin/cos; Pythagorean identities; compound-angle formulae for sin, cos and tan; double-angle formulae; principal values of sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹ and tan⁻¹.
- **Differentiation:** xⁿ, ln x, eˣ, sin, cos, tan, sec, cosec, cot, tan⁻¹x; product and quotient rules; parametric dy/dx = (dy/dt)÷(dx/dt).
- **Integration:**
  - xⁿ (n≠−1), 1/x, eˣ, sin, cos, sec²;
  - 1/(x²+a²) → (1/a)tan⁻¹(x/a);
  - 1/(x²−a²) → (1/2a)ln((x−a)/(x+a)) for x>a;
  - 1/(a²−x²) → (1/2a)ln((a+x)/(a−x)) for |x|<a;
  - integration by parts; ∫f′/f = ln|f|.
- **Vectors:** a·b = a₁b₁+a₂b₂+a₃b₃ = |a||b|cosθ.

**MF19, FURTHER PURE MATHEMATICS section** — [2026–27 syllabus pp.35–36](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697357-2026-2027-syllabus.pdf); [MF19](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/417318-list-of-formulae-and-statistical-tables.pdf)
- **Algebra, summations:** Σr = ½n(n+1); Σr² = (1/6)n(n+1)(2n+1); Σr³ = ¼n²(n+1)².
- **Maclaurin's series:**
  - the general form f(x) = f(0) + x f′(0) + x²/2! f″(0) + … + xʳ/r! f⁽ʳ⁾(0) + …;
  - eˣ (all x); ln(1+x) (−1<x≤1); sin x and cos x (all x); tan⁻¹x (−1≤x≤1); sinh x and cosh x (all x); tanh⁻¹x (−1<x<1).
- **Trigonometry:** if t = tan ½x, then sin x = 2t/(1+t²) and cos x = (1−t²)/(1+t²).
- **Hyperbolic functions:**
  - cosh²x − sinh²x ≡ 1; sinh 2x ≡ 2 sinh x cosh x; cosh 2x ≡ cosh²x + sinh²x;
  - sinh⁻¹x = ln(x+√(x²+1)); cosh⁻¹x = ln(x+√(x²−1)) for x≥1; tanh⁻¹x = ½ln((1+x)/(1−x)) for |x|<1.
- **Differentiation:** sin⁻¹x → 1/√(1−x²); cos⁻¹x → −1/√(1−x²); sinh x → cosh x; cosh x → sinh x; tanh x → sech²x; sinh⁻¹x → 1/√(1+x²); cosh⁻¹x → 1/√(x²−1); tanh⁻¹x → 1/(1−x²).
- **Integration** (a > 0, constants omitted):
  - sec x → ln|sec x + tan x| = ln|tan(½x + ¼π)| for |x| < ½π;
  - cosec x → −ln|cosec x + cot x| = ln|tan ½x| for 0<x<π;
  - sinh x → cosh x; cosh x → sinh x; sech²x → tanh x;
  - 1/√(a²−x²) → sin⁻¹(x/a) for |x|<a;
  - 1/√(x²−a²) → cosh⁻¹(x/a) for x>a;
  - 1/√(a²+x²) → sinh⁻¹(x/a).

### Inferences
These must be memorised, because I found them neither in MF19 nor given by the syllabus.

**Paper 1**
- Sums and products of roots for quadratics, cubics and quartics; Newton-type power-sum relations (e.g. S₄ − S₂ + 2S₁ + 5S₀ = 0, as used in ER 2023).
- 2×2 and 3×3 determinants and inverses; (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹.
- Standard 2×2 matrices for rotation, reflection in y = x tan θ, enlargement, stretch and shear.
- Polar ↔ Cartesian conversion (x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, r² = x² + y²); area ½∫r² dθ.
- The vector product; the three plane forms; the angle between a line and a plane (sine) and between two planes.
- The perpendicular distance from a point to a plane, |ax₀+by₀+cz₀−d|/√(a²+b²+c²). Not in MF19; ER 2023 notes candidates "often omitted the constant term" when using it.
- The skew-line shortest distance |(b−a)·(d₁×d₂)|/|d₁×d₂|.

**Paper 2**
- The definitions of sech, cosech and coth. Their derivatives are not in MF19.
- The derivatives of sech, cosech, coth and cosh⁻¹; the integral of tanh. Only the sinh, cosh, tanh and inverse forms listed above are given.
- Cayley–Hamilton use; the eigen decomposition QDQ⁻¹.
- Second derivatives for implicit and parametric relations, with d²y/dx² = (d/dt(dy/dx))/(dx/dt) in the parametric case.
- Arc length: Cartesian ∫√(1+(dy/dx)²)dx; parametric ∫√(ẋ²+ẏ²)dt; polar ∫√(r²+(dr/dθ)²)dθ.
- Surface area of revolution: 2π∫y ds about the x-axis, or 2π∫x ds about the y-axis.
- De Moivre's theorem; zⁿ + z⁻ⁿ = 2cos nθ and zⁿ − z⁻ⁿ = 2i sin nθ (ER 2023 expects these); the nth roots of unity e^{2πik/n}.
- Integrating factor e^{∫P dx}; the auxiliary equation; CF forms for real, repeated and complex roots; standard PI trial forms, including the resonance case kx cos 2x shown in the syllabus.
- The general error pattern in examiner reports is not knowing or not applying a needed formula (e.g. the missing ½ in the polar area), so memorisation matters.

## Q3. Paper lengths, marks, weightings and the AS vs A Level split

### Takeaway
Papers 1 and 2 are each **2 hours and 75 marks**, with all questions compulsory.
- Paper 1 has 6–8 questions. It is 60% of the AS Level and 30% of the A Level, and is compulsory for both.
- Paper 2 has 7–9 questions. It is 30% of the A Level, is compulsory for A Level only, and is not part of AS.
- AS = Paper 1 + Paper 3 (Mechanics) **or** Paper 1 + Paper 4 (Probability & Statistics). A Level = all four papers.
- This is unchanged for 2028–2030.

### Cited Findings
- **Paper 1:** "2 hours, 75 marks, 6 to 8 structured questions… Answer all questions… 60% of the AS Level, 30% of the A Level, Compulsory for AS Level and A Level" — [2026–27 syllabus p.11](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697357-2026-2027-syllabus.pdf)
- **Paper 2:** "2 hours, 75 marks, 7 to 9 structured questions… 30% of the A Level, Compulsory for A Level" — [2026–27 syllabus p.11](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697357-2026-2027-syllabus.pdf)
- **Papers 3 and 4:** each 1 h 30 min and 50 marks; 40% of the AS Level and 20% of the A Level — [2026–27 syllabus p.11](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697357-2026-2027-syllabus.pdf)

## Q4. Common errors in recent examiner reports for Papers 1 and 2

### Takeaway
The most recent Principal Examiner Report I could read in full is **June 2023**. The 2024–2026 reports are probably published, but the Cambridge site was under maintenance and the mirrors returned 404. Recurring themes:
- Not showing every step for "show that" / given answers, e.g. a characteristic equation must visibly come from a determinant.
- Not reading the question: answering for invariant lines instead of invariant points; giving the sum instead of the equation.
- Incomplete sketches: the pole, asymptotes, intersections and the point furthest from the pole.
- Wrong order of matrix products or inverses.
- Loose hypotheses in induction proofs.
- Off-by-one counting in method of differences.
- Sign errors in eigenvectors.
- Poor notation in differential-equation answers.

### Cited Findings
All findings below are from the [June 2023 Principal Examiner Report for 9231, mirror copy](https://pastpapers.co/cie/A-Level/Mathematics-Further-9231/2023-May-June/9231_s23_er.pdf). Official URL as listed: https://cambridgeinternational.org/Images/567737-june-2023-examiner-report.pdf

**Paper 1 (9231/12 and 9231/13)**
- **Key messages (9231/12 and /13):**
  - read every question carefully;
  - show all steps, especially when proving a given result;
  - label sketched graphs fully, showing significant points and behaviour at limits;
  - (9231/13 only) be "familiar with the formula sheet (MF 19)".
- **Induction (12 Q1, 13 Q1):**
  - The base case must link to the statement at n=1.
  - Some candidates tried to divide by 2 or add the matrices.
  - The hypothesis is often incomplete: candidates must assume "5^{3k}+3^{2k−3} is divisible by 31 for some positive integer k", not that the expression "is true".
  - Candidates who use the difference between terms must explain how it gives divisibility of the (k+1)th term.
- **Inverse of Aⁿ (12 Q1b):** methods other than writing Aⁿ first "often ended with an extra factor of 2".
- **Roots of polynomials (12 Q2, 13 Q3):**
  - Some put in power-sum results before eliminating α, β, γ, which made the algebra harder.
  - Some found only the sum of squares but not the quartic, or the reverse.
- **Summation / method of differences (12 Q3, 13 Q2):**
  - Some forgot to restore a factor 1/k that they took out.
  - Common: subtracting the sum to n terms instead of n−1 terms; miscounting the terms from n+1 to 2n.
- **Matrix transformations (12 Q4, 13 Q4):**
  - Some forgot that θ can be reflex.
  - "The most common error was to get the order of the matrices wrong."
  - Some combined the matrices and then inverted, instead of using (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹.
  - Some read "det(M−I)=0" as M singular or det M = 1.
  - "Most candidates found the invariant lines of the transformation rather than the invariant points that the question required."
  - A rotation description must include the centre (the origin).
- **Polar curves (12 Q5):**
  - The pole must be clearly marked.
  - The coordinates of the point furthest from the pole were often left out or given in the wrong order.
  - "A few missed the ½" in the area formula.
- **Rational functions (12 Q6):**
  - Few spotted that a positive derivative means no turning points.
  - For |f(x)| inequalities, the solution x = 0 was often left out. Candidates who worked with inequalities throughout "often wrote down overlapping regions".
- **Vectors (12 Q7):**
  - Cross-product slips.
  - Finding a point common to two planes was hard for many.
  - The vector equation of a line rarely started "r =".
  - With the point–plane distance formula, candidates "often omitted the constant term".
  - The modulus was equated to a negative quantity.

**Paper 2 (9231/22 and 9231/23)**
- **Key messages:**
  - show all steps, especially for given results;
  - read questions carefully, "particularly when an answer is required in a certain form or in terms of a given variable";
  - use earlier results and MF19.
- **General:** candidates "jumped to conclusions without justification, particularly where answers were given within the question".
- **Linear systems (22 Q1, 23 Q8a):**
  - The geometrical interpretation needs a specific statement, e.g. a sheaf. "'three planes intersect at a point' was insufficient"; candidates must say the planes meet at exactly one point.
  - The efficient method is to show det ≠ 0.
- **Eigenvalues and eigenvectors (22 Q5, 23 Q8b–d):**
  - Writing down the factorised characteristic equation without showing the determinant expansion "gained no credit".
  - Some did not state the eigenvalues after finding the equation.
  - Sign errors were common with the vector-product method; candidates should check with Ae = λe.
  - With Cayley–Hamilton, sign errors were common when expanding brackets.
- **Substitution differential equations (22 Q2):** some did not separate the variables afterwards, or did not give the required form.
- **De Moivre (22 Q3, 23 Q3):** candidates must show they used zⁿ+z⁻ⁿ = 2cos nθ and z−z⁻¹ = 2i sin θ; "it was sometimes not apparent that they knew these rules".
- **Reduction formulae (22 Q4, 23 Q7):**
  - Success came from rewriting x⁵ as (1+x⁵)−1, or x² as (1+x²)−1.
  - Candidates who integrated by parts instead "were unsuccessful".
- **Second-order differential equations (22 Q6, 23 Q2):**
  - Inaccuracy when comparing coefficients for the PI.
  - Giving "expressions instead of equations".
  - Using "an arrow instead of an equals sign".
- **Rectangles and bounds (22 Q7, 23 Q6):** good answers justified the inequality from the sum of the rectangle areas and adapted the method for the lower bound.
- **Hyperbolic identities (22 Q8a, 23 Q5a):** candidates should "work on just one side at a time".
- **Parametric second derivative (22 Q8c):** candidates forgot to divide by dx/dt after differentiating with respect to t. Sign errors in the first derivative were common.
- **Implicit differentiation and Maclaurin (23 Q1, Q4):**
  - Sign errors, or a lost factor of x, in the product rule for the third derivative.
  - Expanding (x+y)⁶ before differentiating "should be discouraged".
  - Losing the minus sign on the final answer.
- **Integrating factor (23 Q5b):** candidates who divided by cosh²x before finding the constant lost accuracy.
- **Arc length (23 Q7a,c):** some got ln 2 instead of ln 3 through careless log rules. Good answers started from the arc-length formula and changed the limits.

### Inferences
- The same key messages appear year after year: show steps for given results, read the question, use MF19, sketch with full detail. This suggests they are stable marking priorities, not one-off comments.
- Topics that cost marks repeatedly:
  - matrix order and invariant points vs lines (P1);
  - induction hypothesis wording (P1);
  - method-of-differences term counts (P1);
  - eigenvector signs and a visible determinant derivation (P2);
  - parametric second derivatives (P2);
  - differential-equation notation: write y = …, not an expression (P2).
